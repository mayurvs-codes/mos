U3-Q1Q1 | L1 Remember | Deflection of beams

Write the differential equation of the elastic curve and define flexural rigidity. State the boundary conditions for a cantilever and for a simply supported beam.

Solution

Definition. The elastic curve (deflection curve) is the curve into which the axis of a beam deforms under transverse load. Its slope is θ≈dv/dx\theta\approx dv/dx and its curvature is κ=1/r=d2v/dx2\kappa=1/r=d^2v/dx^2.

Principle. For a linearly elastic beam, curvature is proportional to bending moment: κ=M/EI\kappa=M/EI. Equating this with the geometric curvature gives the differential equation.

Differential equation of the elastic curve

d2vdx2=M(x)EI\dfrac{d^{2}v}{dx^{2}}=\dfrac{M(x)}{EI}

Flexural rigidity. The product EIEI (modulus of elasticity times second moment of area of the section about the neutral axis) is the flexural rigidity. It measures a beam's resistance to bending: a larger EIEI gives a smaller deflection for the same moment. Its SI unit is N m2^2.

Sign convention. xx to the right and vv upward positive; slope positive counter-clockwise; MM positive when it causes compression in the upper part of the beam.

Boundary conditions (they fix the two constants of integration):

  • Cantilever (fixed at A): v=0v=0 and dv/dx=0dv/dx=0 at the fixed end.
  • Simply supported beam (pin at A, roller at B): v=0v=0 at both supports; the slopes at the supports are not zero.
  • Symmetry condition (when the loading is symmetric, for example a UDL over the whole span of a simply supported beam): dv/dx=0dv/dx=0 at mid-span.

Conclusion. Integrate EI v′′=MEI\,v''=M twice and fix the two constants from the boundary conditions to get slope and deflection.

U3-Q2Q2 | L1 Remember | Columns

Write Euler's buckling formula and give the effective length for the four standard end conditions.

Solution

Definition. Buckling is the sudden sideways bowing of a slender column under axial compressive load. The smallest axial load at which a straight column can stay slightly bent is the critical (Euler) load PcrP_{cr}.

Euler's formula

PcrP_{cr}​=π2EIminLe2=\dfrac{\pi^{2}EI_{min}}{L_e^{2}}, where LeL_e is the effective length and IminI_{min} the least second moment of area of the section.

Effective lengths (with LL the actual length):

End condition Effective length LeL_e
Both ends hinged (pinned) LL
One end fixed, other free 2L2L
Both ends fixed 0.5L0.5L
One end fixed, other hinged 0.7L0.7L (some problems use L/2L/\sqrt2)

Assumptions. Homogeneous, isotropic, elastic material; uniform section; column initially straight and axially loaded; self weight negligible; failure by buckling alone.

Conclusion. The effective length is the length of the equivalent pinned-pinned column with the same buckling load; a smaller LeL_e means a stronger column.

U3-Q3Q3 | L1 Remember | Torsion

Write the torsion equation and state the assumptions made in deriving it.

Solution

Definition. Torsion is the twisting of a shaft by a torque TT applied about its axis.

Torsion equation

TJ=τr=GθL\dfrac{T}{J}=\dfrac{\tau}{r}=\dfrac{G\theta}{L}

Here TT is the torque (N mm), JJ the polar moment of inertia of the section (mm4^4), τ\tau the shear stress at radius rr, GG the modulus of rigidity, θ\theta the angle of twist (radians) and LL the length. The maximum stress is at the surface: τmax=TR/J\tau_{max}=TR/J.

Assumptions in the theory of pure torsion.

  1. The material is homogeneous and isotropic.
  2. Stresses are within the elastic limit: shear stress is proportional to shear strain.
  3. Plane cross-sections remain plane after twisting (no warping).
  4. Radial lines remain radial.
  5. The twist along the shaft is uniform.

Conclusion. Shear stress grows linearly from zero at the axis to the maximum at the surface; the torsion equation is used both for the stress (strength) and for the twist (stiffness) of a shaft.

U3-Q4Q4 | L2 Understand | Deflection by singularity functions

Explain singularity (Macaulay) functions and the rules for using them to find the slope and deflection of beams with discontinuous loading.

Solution

Definition. A singularity (Macaulay) function is ⟨x−x0⟩n\langle x-x_0\rangle^{n} with n≥0n\ge0. It equals (x−x0)n(x-x_0)^n when x≥x0x\ge x_0 and is zero when x≤x0x\le x_0.

Principle. Double integration needs a separate bending-moment expression for each portion of a beam with several loads, which brings extra constants and continuity equations. Singularity functions let one expression M(x)M(x) describe the whole beam: each load starts "switching on" at its own position x0x_0.

Rules.

  1. Write M(x)M(x) about the section xx taking the forces to its left. Each load gets a bracket: point load PP at x1x_1: −P⟨x−x1⟩1-P\langle x-x_1\rangle^{1}; applied couple MAM_A at x2x_2: +MA⟨x−x2⟩0+M_A\langle x-x_2\rangle^{0}; UDL ww starting at x3x_3: −w2⟨x−x3⟩2-\dfrac{w}{2}\langle x-x_3\rangle^{2}; reactions with their own brackets.
  2. A UDL that stops before the end of the beam must be continued to the end and cancelled by an equal and opposite load starting where it stops.
  3. Put M(x)M(x) into EI v′′=M(x)EI\,v''=M(x).
  4. Integrate twice. Brackets integrate as ∫⟨x−x0⟩ndx\int\langle x-x_0\rangle^{n}dx​=⟨x−x0⟩n+1n+1=\dfrac{\langle x-x_0\rangle^{n+1}}{n+1} (the bracket is kept). Add the constants C1C_1 (slope equation) and C2C_2 (deflection equation) once only.
  5. Find C1,C2C_1,C_2 from the support conditions (v=0v=0 at pins and rollers; v=0v=0 and v′=0v'=0 at a fixed end).
  6. For the maximum deflection set v′=0v'=0, solve for xx, check it lies in the portion assumed (drop brackets whose start is beyond xx), then evaluate vv.

Formula example. M(x)M(x)​=RLx=R_Lx​−P⟨x-P\langle x​−x1⟩-x_1\rangle​+MA⟨x+M_A\langle x​−x2⟩0-x_2\rangle^{0}​−w⟨x−x3⟩22-\dfrac{w\langle x-x_3\rangle^{2}}{2} (lecture notes N 54).

Applications and conclusion. The method is most useful for beams with several point loads, couples and partial UDLs, where it needs only two constants whatever the number of loads.

U3-Q5Q5 | L2 Understand | Columns: limits of Euler, Rankine

Explain the limitations of Euler's formula. Why is an empirical formula such as Rankine-Gordon used in column design? Explain the slenderness ratio and the limiting slenderness ratio.

Solution

Slenderness ratio. λ=Lek\lambda=\dfrac{L_e}{k} where k=Imin/Ak=\sqrt{I_{min}/A} is the least radius of gyration. It measures how slender a column is: large λ\lambda means long and thin.

Euler's formula and its limitation. Euler's critical stress is σcr=π2Eλ2\sigma_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^{2}E}{\lambda^{2}}. It assumes failure by pure buckling with elastic behaviour. As λ→0\lambda\to0 the formula would give an infinite stress, which is impossible: the material crushes at its crushing stress σc\sigma_c before that. So Euler's formula is valid only for long columns, where σcr≤σc\sigma_{cr}\le\sigma_c. It also assumes a perfectly straight column, a perfectly axial load, perfect end conditions and no self weight, which real columns do not meet.

Limiting slenderness ratio. Setting σcr=σc\sigma_{cr}=\sigma_c:

(Lek)lim\left(\dfrac{L_e}{k}\right)_{lim}​=π2Eσc=\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi^{2}E}{\sigma_c}}. Euler is valid only if Lek>(Lek)lim\dfrac{L_e}{k}>\left(\dfrac{L_e}{k}\right)_{lim}.

Example from the lecture slides: steel with E=2×105E=2\times10^{5} N/mm2^2 and σc=320\sigma_c=320 N/mm2^2 gives λlim=78.5\lambda_{lim}=78.5.

Why an empirical formula is used. Short columns crush and long columns buckle, but real intermediate columns fail by a mix of both. Euler fails there. Rankine-Gordon combines the two loads as 1PR=1Pc+1PE\dfrac1{P_R}=\dfrac1{P_c}+\dfrac1{P_E} with Pc=σcAP_c=\sigma_cA the crushing load and PEP_E the Euler load, giving

PRP_R​=σcA1+a(Le/k)2=\dfrac{\sigma_cA}{1+a\left(L_e/k\right)^{2}},   a=σcπ2E\;a=\dfrac{\sigma_c}{\pi^{2}E} (Rankine's constant)

For a short column PEP_E is large and PR→PcP_R\to P_c; for a long column PcP_c is large compared with the buckling effect and PR→PEP_R\to P_E. So one formula works for all lengths. Typical constants from the slides: mild steel σc=320\sigma_c=320 N/mm2^2, a=1/7500a=1/7500; cast iron 550 and 1/1600; wrought iron 250 and 1/9000; timber 50 and 1/750.

Classification by slenderness (as given in the slides). Long: Le/k>50L_e/k>50 (C06 p9) or, in the later slides, Le/k>120L_e/k>120; short: <50<50 (C06) or <32<32 (C08). The two slide sets differ; quote the one your lecturer uses.

Conclusion. Use Euler for long columns, crushing for short ones and Rankine-Gordon for intermediate columns (and as a safe estimate generally).

U3-Q6Q6 | L2 Understand | Torsion: hollow vs solid shaft

Explain why a hollow shaft is more efficient than a solid shaft of the same material and weight in transmitting torque.

Solution

Principle. In torsion the shear stress is τ=Tr/J\tau=Tr/J: zero at the axis and largest at the surface. The material near the axis is lightly stressed and contributes little to the torque capacity, but it adds weight. Removing it and putting the same material farther out raises the polar moment of inertia.

Formulas. Solid shaft of diameter dd: Js=π32d4J_s=\dfrac{\pi}{32}d^{4}, Zp=π16d3Z_p=\dfrac{\pi}{16}d^{3}. Hollow shaft of outer and inner diameters DoD_o, DiD_i: Jh=π32(Do4−Di4)J_h=\dfrac{\pi}{32}(D_o^{4}-D_i^{4}), Zp=π16Do4−Di4DoZ_p=\dfrac{\pi}{16}\dfrac{D_o^{4}-D_i^{4}}{D_o}. Torque capacity at a given maximum stress: T=τmaxZpT=\tau_{max}Z_p. Angle of twist: θ=TL/GJ\theta=TL/GJ.

Explanation. Same material and same weight (and length) means the same cross-sectional area: π4(Do2−Di2)\dfrac\pi4(D_o^2-D_i^2)​=π4d2=\dfrac\pi4d^2. Then

JhJs=Do4−Di4d4=(Do2−Di2)(Do2+Di2)d4=Do2+Di2d2>1.\begin{aligned}\frac{J_h}{J_s}&=\frac{D_o^{4}-D_i^{4}}{d^{4}}=\frac{(D_o^2-D_i^2)(D_o^2+D_i^2)}{d^{4}} \\ &=\frac{D_o^{2}+D_i^{2}}{d^{2}}>1.\end{aligned}JhJs=Do4−Di4d4=(Do2−Di2)(Do2+Di2)d4=Do2+Di2d2>1.\begin{aligned}\frac{J_h}{J_s}&=\frac{D_o^{4}-D_i^{4}}{d^{4}} \\ &=\frac{(D_o^2-D_i^2)(D_o^2+D_i^2)}{d^{4}} \\ &=\frac{D_o^{2}+D_i^{2}}{d^{2}}>1.\end{aligned}

So Jh>JsJ_h>J_s always, and the hollow shaft is stiffer (smaller twist) and stronger for the same weight.

Numerical illustration. Take Di=0.6DoD_i=0.6D_o. Equal area gives Do2(1−0.36)=d2D_o^2(1-0.36)=d^2, so Do=1.25dD_o=1.25d. Then Jh/Js=2.125J_h/J_s=2.125 (twist is 1/2.1251/2.125 as much) and Zp,h/Zp,s=1.70Z_{p,h}/Z_{p,s}=1.70 (70 % more torque at the same maximum stress). (Computed in code.)

Applications and conclusion. This is why drive shafts and propeller shafts are made hollow: more strength and stiffness for the same weight. The limit is that a very thin wall may buckle or wrinkle locally and is more costly to make.
