A. Deflection of beams

A1. What is deflection and why do we care

A beam carrying a load does not stay straight. It bends. The distance a point on the beam axis moves in the y direction (across the beam) is the deflection of that point. The curve the axis becomes is the deflection curve or elastic curve (C01 p4).

Why bother (C01 p3): to check that deflections stay within tolerable limits, to analyse statically indeterminate structures, and for dynamic problems such as aircraft wings and buildings in earthquakes.

Everyday example. A diving board bends down when someone stands on its tip. The drop of the tip is the deflection. How much the board tilts at the tip is the slope. A board that dips too much feels unsafe even if it does not break, so deflection is limited.

A2. Slope, curvature and the differential equation

The slides (C01 p5-9, N 5-9) use vv for deflection and θ\theta for the slope angle. Follow the steps.

  1. Take two points a1a_1 at distance xx and a2a_2 at x+dxx+dx on the curve. Their deflections are vv and v+dvv+dv. The tangent angles are θ\theta and θ+dθ\theta+d\theta.
  2. If rr is the radius of curvature, the small arc is ds=r dθds = r\,d\theta, so the curvature is κ=1/r=dθ/ds\kappa = 1/r = d\theta/ds.
  3. The slope is dv/dx=tan⁡θdv/dx = \tan\theta. For small θ\theta: cos⁡θ≈1\cos\theta\approx 1, ds≈dxds\approx dx and tan⁡θ≈θ\tan\theta\approx\theta. So θ≈dv/dx\theta \approx dv/dx and κ\kappa ​=dθ/dx= d\theta/dx ​=d2v/dx2= d^2v/dx^2.
  4. For a linearly elastic beam obeying Hooke's law the curvature is also κ=1/r=M/EI\kappa = 1/r = M/EI.
  5. Equate step 3 and step 4.

Sign convention (C01 p10, N 10). Write this before every problem.

  • xx is positive to the right and yy (and vv) positive upward.
  • Slope dv/dxdv/dx and rotation θ\theta are positive when counter-clockwise from the +x+x axis.
  • Bending moment MM is positive when it produces compression in the upper part of the beam (sagging).

Units. MM in N mm, EE in N/mm2^2 (1 GPa = 1000 N/mm2^2), II in mm4^4, vv in mm.

A3. Double integration method

The idea (C01 p11-13, N 11-13): the equation is second order, so integrate twice.

  1. Find the support reactions and write M(x)M(x) for each portion of the beam (a new expression whenever the load changes).
  2. Put M(x)M(x) in EI d2v/dx2=M(x)EI\,d^2v/dx^2 = M(x).
  3. Integrate once: EI dv/dxEI\,dv/dx ​=∫M dx+C1= \int M\,dx + C_1. This is the slope equation.
  4. Integrate again: EI vEI\,v​=∬M dx dx+C1x+C2=\iint M\,dx\,dx + C_1x + C_2. This is the deflection equation.
  5. Find C1,C2C_1, C_2 from conditions at known points.

Boundary conditions are about the supports (C01 p12): at a pin or roller the deflection is zero (v=0v=0) but the slope is not; at a fixed support both are zero (v=0v=0 and dv/dx=0dv/dx=0).

Symmetry conditions (C01 p13): for a simply supported beam with a UDL over the whole span, the slope at mid-span is zero, (dv/dx)x=L/2=0(dv/dx)_{x=L/2}=0, because the deflected shape is symmetric.

Difficult step: a beam with several portions. Each portion has its own pair of constants, so you get 4, 6, ... constants. Then you also use continuity: at the joint between two portions the slope and the deflection from both expressions must be equal. The singularity-function method (topic A6, later) writes one equation for the whole beam and avoids this.

Why two methods. Double integration suits a beam with one or two load portions (standard cases A4, worked problems A5). Singularity functions suit loading with many steps or point loads. Moment-area is not in the Unit 3 notes or slides, so it is not used here.

A4. Four standard cases by double integration

The slides (C02 p3-17, N 14-35) work out four cases fully. Learn the pattern; the answers become formulas you can quote. The slides write yy for the deflection and WW or ww for the load; ll (here LL) is the span.

Difficult steps.

  • Why the cantilever moment is negative. The load pulls the free end down, so the beam is hogging (tension on top). By the slide convention, moment is positive only when the top is in compression, so M=−WxM=-Wx.
  • Why the slope at a simple support is not zero. A pin or roller only stops vertical movement, so the beam end is free to rotate. Only a fixed support stops rotation.
  • Why we use x=L/2x=L/2 in the SS cases. The loading is symmetric, so the lowest point is at mid-span where the tangent is horizontal. This gives the second condition without needing a second moment expression for the right half.
  • Signs. If the slope or deflection comes out negative, it means clockwise or downward under the slide convention. Quote magnitudes when the question asks for "maximum deflection" and say the direction.

A6. Singularity (Macaulay) functions

Why we need them (N 52, C03 p9). Double integration is quick only when one formula for M(x)M(x) covers the whole beam. A beam with several loads needs one MM expression per portion, and every extra portion adds two constants and two continuity equations. Singularity functions let us write one bending moment equation for the entire beam and keep just two constants C1,C2C_1,C_2.

Definition (N 53). For n≥0n\ge 0:

Singularity function

⟨x−x0⟩n\langle x-x_0\rangle^{n}​=(x−x0)n=(x-x_0)^{n} when x≥x0x\ge x_0, and ⟨x−x0⟩n=0\langle x-x_0\rangle^{n}=0 when x≤x0x\le x_0.

Integration

∫⟨x−x0⟩n dx\displaystyle\int\langle x-x_0\rangle^{n}\,dx​=⟨x−x0⟩n+1n+1=\dfrac{\langle x-x_0\rangle^{n+1}}{n+1} (the bracket is kept when we integrate).

In words: the bracket "switches on" at x0x_0. Before x0x_0 it is zero, so that load does not contribute to the moment.

Everyday example. Think of a shop price list: "pay 5 per kg for everything over 3 kg". Below 3 kg the extra charge is zero; above it the extra charge grows. The bracket ⟨x−3⟩\langle x-3\rangle is the "kg over 3".

Writing M(x)M(x) with one equation (N 54). Take the moment of all forces to the left of the section. For the beam in the notes (left reaction RLR_L, point load PP at x1x_1, couple MAM_A at x2x_2, UDL ww starting at x3x_3):

M(x)=RLx−P⟨x−x1⟩1+MA⟨x−x2⟩0−w⟨x−x3⟩22.M(x)=R_Lx-P\langle x-x_1\rangle^{1}+M_A\langle x-x_2\rangle^{0}-\frac{w\langle x-x_3\rangle^{2}}{2}.M(x)=RLx−P⟨x−x1⟩1+MA⟨x−x2⟩0−w⟨x−x3⟩22.\begin{aligned}M(x)&=R_Lx-P\langle x-x_1\rangle^{1} \\ &\quad +M_A\langle x-x_2\rangle^{0} \\ &\quad -\frac{w\langle x-x_3\rangle^{2}}{2}.\end{aligned}

Each load gets its own bracket whose power is one more than the load's "shape": a point load gives power 1 (moment grows linearly), a couple gives power 0 (a constant jump) and a UDL gives power 2 (moment grows with the square).

Steps of the singularity method (as used in N 55-77).

  1. Find the reactions.
  2. Write one M(x)M(x) with a bracket for each load. A UDL that does not run to the end of the beam must be continued to the end and then cancelled (see the difficult step below).
  3. EI y′′=M(x)EI\,y''=M(x). Integrate twice, integrating brackets by the rule above, adding C1C_1 and C2C_2 once.
  4. Use the support conditions (for example y=0y=0 at both supports of a simply supported beam, y=y′=0y=y'=0 at a fixed end) to find C1,C2C_1,C_2.
  5. For the maximum deflection set the slope y′=0y'=0, solve for xx (decide which portion that xx lies in), then put that xx in the deflection equation.

Difficult step: a UDL that stops before the end of the beam. The bracket ⟨x−3⟩2\langle x-3\rangle^{2} never switches off. If the UDL ends at xex_e you add an equal and opposite load starting at xex_e ("continue and cancel"). In the notes' first example the UDL runs from C to the end D, so no cancelling is needed.

Difficult step: which brackets are active. When you evaluate at a point xx remember to drop every bracket whose start is larger than xx (it is zero there). Most arithmetic slips happen here.

B. Columns

B1. What is a column and how does it fail

A column is any member subjected to axial compressive load (C06 p5). The loads are applied at the ends and produce axial compressive stress. Examples on the slides: building columns, bridge piers, the column of a planer or drilling machine (C06 p6-7).

Everyday example. Press down on the end of a plastic ruler held upright. A short ruler just gets squeezed. A long thin ruler suddenly bows sideways. That sideways bowing is buckling.

Classification by nature of failure (C06 p8):

  1. Short column: fails by direct compressive stress only (crushing).
  2. Medium column: fails by a combination of direct compressive stress and buckling (bending stress).
  3. Long column: fails by buckling; the direct compressive stress plays a negligible role.

Classification by slenderness (C06 p9-10). Here LL is the effective length and kk the minimum radius of gyration.

  • Long column: L/k>50L/k>50.
  • Short column: L/k<50L/k<50, or L/d<15L/d<15. The load at which a short column fails by crushing is the crushing load.

Flag: two sets of limits. Later in the course material (C08 p4) the limits are given differently: short if Le/k<32L_e/k<32, long if Le/k>120L_e/k>120, intermediate between 32 and 120. The two slide sets do not agree. In answers, quote the limit and say which slide set it comes from.

B2. Critical load and stability

The slides (C06 p13-14) use a model of two rigid bars joined by a rotational spring, loaded by an axial force PP. When PP is small the straight position is stable: if you push it sideways it comes back. When PP is large the straight position is unstable: a tiny push makes it collapse sideways. The transition value is the critical load PcrP_{cr}.

Stability test

if P<PcrP<P_{cr} the structure is stable; if P>PcrP>P_{cr} it is unstable (C06 p14).

B3. Support types and end conditions

The slides recall the four support types (C06 p12): roller (vertical reaction), pinned (horizontal and vertical reaction), fixed (horizontal, vertical and moment), simple support (vertical reaction).

The end conditions of a column (C06 p15-16):

  1. Both ends hinged (pinned).
  2. One end fixed and the other free.
  3. Both ends fixed.
  4. One end fixed and the other hinged.

The effective length LeL_e is the length of an equivalent column with both ends hinged, of the same material and cross-section, that has the same buckling load (C08 p2). In the slide figure (C06 p16):

B4. Euler's assumptions, slenderness ratio, limits of Euler's formula, Rankine

Assumptions in Euler's theory (C06 p17).

  • (a) The material is homogeneous, isotropic and elastic.
  • (b) The section is uniform throughout.
  • (c) The column is initially straight and loaded axially.
  • (d) Self weight is negligible.
  • (e) The column fails by buckling alone.

C. Torsion of circular shafts

C1. What is torsion and what do we assume

Torsion is twisting of a member by a torque (twisting moment) TT about its axis. Shafts that carry power in machines are the main example.

Everyday example. Wring a wet towel: the cloth twists about its length. Turn a screwdriver: the shank is in torsion.

The slides (C09 p3) note two things: the net of the internal shear stresses must equal the applied torque, T=∫ρ dF=∫ρ(τ dA)T=\int\rho\,dF=\int\rho(\tau\,dA), but how τ\tau is distributed is statically indeterminate. Unlike the uniform stress of axial load, torsional shear stress cannot be assumed uniform; we must look at how the shaft deforms.

Assumptions in the theory of pure torsion (C09 p4).

  1. The material is homogeneous and isotropic.
  2. The stresses are within the elastic limit: shear stress is proportional to shear strain.
  3. Cross-sections that are plane before twisting stay plane after twisting (no warping).
  4. Radial lines remain radial after twisting.
  5. The twist along the shaft is uniform.

C2. Deriving the torsion equation

Follow the slides (C09 p5-11). Take a shaft of length LL and radius RR, fixed at one end, torque TT at the other.

  1. A line ABAB parallel to the axis on the surface turns to AB′AB'. Let ϕ\phi be the shear strain (angle BAB′BAB') and θ\theta the angle of twist over length LL (radians). The arc BB′BB' is Rθ=LϕR\theta=L\phi.
  2. Hooke's law in shear: ϕ=τsG\phi=\dfrac{\tau_s}{G}, with τs\tau_s the surface shear stress. So Rθ=LτsGR\theta=L\dfrac{\tau_s}{G}, i.e. τsR=GθL\dfrac{\tau_s}{R}=\dfrac{G\theta}{L} (1).
  3. The same argument for a point at distance rr from the centre: τr=GθL\dfrac{\tau}{r}=\dfrac{G\theta}{L}. Hence τsR=τr\dfrac{\tau_s}{R}=\dfrac\tau r (2): the shear stress grows linearly with rr, from zero at the axis to the maximum τs\tau_s at the surface.

From TJ=GθL\dfrac TJ=\dfrac{G\theta}{L} we get T=GJLθT=\dfrac{GJ}{L}\theta (C09 p16). The product GJGJ is the torsional rigidity (stiffness) of the shaft: the torque needed to produce unit angle of twist in unit length. It is analogous to the flexural rigidity EIEI in bending.

C8. Torsion worked problems: hollow shafts and composite shafts (C11)

Recipe for twist in shafts with several portions: for each portion find its internal torque TiT_i, length LiL_i, modulus GiG_i and polar moment JiJ_i; the twist of that portion is ϕi=TiLiGiJi\phi_i=\dfrac{T_iL_i}{G_iJ_i}.
