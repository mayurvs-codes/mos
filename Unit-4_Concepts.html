A. Transformation of stresses

A1. Why transform stresses

The stresses you already know, σ=P/A\sigma=P/A, τ=T/J\tau=T/J and σ=My/I\sigma=My/I, act on a particular plane: the cross-section. Real parts fail on other planes. A mild-steel bar in tension shows slip bands at about 45∘45^{\circ} to the load; a timber block fails in shear along the grain; a weld seam or glue joint sits at an angle; chalk twisted in torsion cracks along a 45∘45^{\circ} helix (N 2).

Everyday example. Pull a sheet of paper apart at a slant and it tears along a slanted line, not straight across. The tearing plane is where the stress is worst, and it is generally not the plane you were pulling on.

The physical state of stress at a point does not change when we rotate our axes, but the numbers describing it do. Transformation of stress converts the components (σx,σy,τxy)(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\tau_{xy}) known in one set of axes to (σx′,σy′,τx′y′)(\sigma_{x'},\sigma_{y'},\tau_{x'y'}) in another. Design uses the extreme values: the principal stresses and the maximum shear stress.

A2. Plane stress and the sign convention

Definition. A point is in plane stress in the xyxy-plane when only the xx and yy faces of an element carry stress and the stresses on the zz faces are zero: σz=τxz=τyz=0\sigma_z=\tau_{xz}=\tau_{yz}=0. Thin plates loaded in their plane, and the free surface of any loaded body, are examples (N 2).

Sign convention (Beer and Johnston, as used in class; N 3):

  1. Normal stress σ\sigma: tension positive, compression negative. The subscript names the face it acts on.
  2. Shear stress τxy\tau_{xy}: first subscript is the face (normal to xx), second is the direction (yy). It is positive when it acts on a positive face in the positive direction of an axis, or on a negative face in the negative direction.
  3. Angle θ\theta: positive when measured counter-clockwise from the xx axis to the x′x' axis.
  4. Moment equilibrium of the element gives τxy=τyx\tau_{xy}=\tau_{yx}.

Difficult step: a caution about Mohr's circle. The positive τxy\tau_{xy} above produces a counter-clockwise couple on the element. Mohr's circle (topic A7) plots clockwise-turning shear upward, so the sign of τxy\tau_{xy} is reversed when the point XX is plotted (N 3).

A5. Principal stresses and principal planes

σx′\sigma_{x'} and τx′y′\tau_{x'y'} change continuously with θ\theta; design needs their extreme values (N 5-6, C01 p15-20).

A6. Maximum shear stress

  1. Set dτx′y′/dθ=0d\tau_{x'y'}/d\theta=0 in A.4: −(σx−σy)cos⁡2θ−2τxysin⁡2θ-(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\cos2\theta-2\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta​=0=0, so tan⁡2θs=−σx−σy2τxy\tan2\theta_s=-\dfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2\tau_{xy}} (A.9).

A8. Mohr's circle

Idea. Otto Mohr showed that the transformation equations are the parametric equations of a circle. Instead of remembering formulas, you draw a picture and read all the stresses from it (N 8).

Derivation (A.8.1, N 9). Write A.3 and A.4 with σn=σx′\sigma_n=\sigma_{x'} and τ=τx′y′\tau=\tau_{x'y'}, move the constant term of A.3 to the left, square both equations and add. The sin⁡2θcos⁡2θ\sin2\theta\cos2\theta terms cancel and sin⁡2+cos⁡2=1\sin^2+\cos^2=1:

(σn−σx+σy2)2+τ2=(σx−σy2)2+τxy2.\left(\sigma_n-\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau^2=\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2.(σn−σx+σy2)2+τ2=(σx−σy2)2+τxy2.\begin{aligned}&\left(\sigma_n-\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau^2 \\ &=\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2 \\ &\quad +\tau_{xy}^2.\end{aligned}

This is a circle in the σ\sigma-τ\tau plane:

Centre and radius (A.12)

centre C=(σx+σy2, 0)C=\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2},\,0\right), radius R=(σx−σy2)2+τxy2R=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^{2}+\tau_{xy}^{2}}

Construction rules (N 9, Beer and Johnston / class convention).

  1. Draw σ\sigma (horizontal, tension to the right) and τ\tau (vertical) axes. Shear that tends to turn the element clockwise is plotted upward.
  2. Plot X=(σx, −τxy)X=(\sigma_x,\,-\tau_{xy}) and Y=(σy, +τxy)Y=(\sigma_y,\,+\tau_{xy}). (A positive τxy\tau_{xy} of the A2 convention turns the element counter-clockwise, so XX lies below the σ\sigma axis.)
  3. Join XX and YY. The line crosses the σ\sigma axis at the centre C=(σx+σy)/2C=(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)/2; it is a diameter.
  4. Draw the circle with centre CC and radius R=CXR=CX.
  5. The points AA and BB where the circle cuts the σ\sigma axis give σ1=OA\sigma_1=OA and σ2=OB\sigma_2=OB. The points DD and EE (top and bottom) give τmax=R\tau_{max}=R with σavg=OC\sigma_{avg}=OC.
  6. Angles: rotating the element by θ\theta corresponds to rotating the diameter by 2θ2\theta in the same sense. The angle XCA=2θpXCA=2\theta_p.
  7. To find the stresses on a plane rotated θ\theta from xx, rotate the diameter XYXY by 2θ2\theta about CC to X′Y′X'Y'. The coordinates of X′X' are (σx′,−τx′y′)(\sigma_{x'},-\tau_{x'y'}) and those of Y′Y' are (σy′,+τx′y′)(\sigma_{y'},+\tau_{x'y'}).

Why angles are doubled. A point on the circle is described by cos⁡2θ\cos2\theta and sin⁡2θ\sin2\theta, because stress is a second-order quantity. Two planes θ\theta apart on the element are 2θ2\theta apart on the circle.

B. Pressure vessels

B1. What is a pressure vessel; thin or thick

A pressure vessel is a closed container that holds a gas or liquid at a pressure very different from the atmosphere: boilers, air receivers, LPG bullets and spheres, hydraulic cylinders, gun barrels, pipelines (N 13). Failure can be catastrophic, so design follows codes, but the stress analysis is what we learn here.

Everyday example. A party balloon and a bicycle tyre: the rubber skin is thin and stretched evenly all round. A hydraulic jack body is thick-walled steel and feels very different stresses on its inside and outside.

B2. Thin-walled cylinder

A closed cylinder of radius rr (inner or mean), wall thickness tt, length LL, under internal gauge pressure pp. Because t≪rt\ll r the stress is taken uniform through the thickness. The wall carries (N 13-14):

  • Hoop (circumferential) stress σh\sigma_h (also σ1\sigma_1 or σθ\sigma_\theta): tends to split the cylinder along a longitudinal seam.
  • Longitudinal (axial) stress σL\sigma_L (also σ2\sigma_2 or σa\sigma_a): tends to tear the cylinder along a circumferential seam.
  • Radial stress at most −p-p on the inside: negligible against σh\sigma_h and σL\sigma_L. So the vessel is in plane (biaxial) stress.

Hoop stress (B.2.1). Cut the cylinder by a plane through its axis and take a length LL of one half. The pressure force on the projected area 2rL2rL is balanced by the hoop force in the two cut walls, each of area tLtL:

p(2rL)=2σh(tL)  ⇒  σh=prt=pD2t.p(2rL)=2\sigma_h(tL)\;\Rightarrow\;\sigma_h=\frac{pr}{t}=\frac{pD}{2t}.p(2rL)=2σh(tL)  ⇒  σh=prt=pD2t.\begin{aligned}p(2rL)&=2\sigma_h(tL)\;\Rightarrow\;\sigma_h=\frac{pr}{t} \\ &=\frac{pD}{2t}.\end{aligned}

Longitudinal stress (B.2.2). Cut by a plane perpendicular to the axis. The pressure acts on the end area πr2\pi r^2 and is resisted by the wall ring of area 2πrt2\pi rt (thin wall):

p(πr2)=σL(2πrt)  ⇒  σL=pr2t=pD4t.p(\pi r^2)=\sigma_L(2\pi rt)\;\Rightarrow\;\sigma_L=\frac{pr}{2t}=\frac{pD}{4t}.p(πr2)=σL(2πrt)  ⇒  σL=pr2t=pD4t.\begin{aligned}p(\pi r^2)&=\sigma_L(2\pi rt)\;\Rightarrow\;\sigma_L=\frac{pr}{2t} \\ &=\frac{pD}{4t}.\end{aligned}

B3. Thin-walled sphere

Cut a sphere of radius rr along a diametral plane. The pressure force on the projected area πr2\pi r^2 is resisted by the wall ring 2πrt2\pi rt (N 15):

p(πr2)=σ(2πrt)  ⇒  σ=pr2t=pD4t.p(\pi r^2)=\sigma(2\pi rt)\;\Rightarrow\;\sigma=\frac{pr}{2t}=\frac{pD}{4t}.p(πr2)=σ(2πrt)  ⇒  σ=pr2t=pD4t.\begin{aligned}p(\pi r^2)&=\sigma(2\pi rt)\;\Rightarrow\;\sigma=\frac{pr}{2t} \\ &=\frac{pD}{4t}.\end{aligned}

By symmetry the stress is the same in every direction in the tangent plane (σ1=σ2=σ\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\sigma). Strain and volume change:

Sphere

σ=pD4t\sigma=\dfrac{pD}{4t},   ε=(1−ν)σE=pr(1−ν)2tE\;\varepsilon=\dfrac{(1-\nu)\sigma}{E}=\dfrac{pr(1-\nu)}{2tE},   ΔVV=3ε=3pr(1−ν)2tE\;\dfrac{\Delta V}{V}=3\varepsilon=\dfrac{3pr(1-\nu)}{2tE}. In-plane Mohr's circle is a point; τabs,max=σ/2\tau_{abs,max}=\sigma/2.

B5. Thick-walled cylinders: Lamé's theory

When t>r/10t>r/10 (the slides use t>d/20t>d/20 for the same idea) the hoop stress is visibly non-uniform and the radial stress cannot be ignored. The radial stress is compressive and the hoop and longitudinal stresses are tensile for internal pressure (N 16, C08 p3). Lamé (1852) found the exact solution.

Everyday example. Think of a gun barrel or a hydraulic jack body: the metal right next to the bore is squeezed hardest and stretched most; the outside barely feels the pressure.

Assumptions (N 16, C08 p4-5).

  1. The material is homogeneous, isotropic and obeys Hooke's law (stresses within the proportional limit).
  2. The cylinder is long, and plane sections normal to the axis stay plane and normal after loading. So the longitudinal strain εL\varepsilon_L (and for uniform loading the longitudinal stress σL\sigma_L) is constant over the section.
  3. The pressures act uniformly (axisymmetry): σr\sigma_r and σθ\sigma_\theta depend on the radius rr only; shear stresses vanish.
  4. Deformation is small.

Notation. The notes use stresses with tension positive: σr\sigma_r (radial) and σθ\sigma_\theta (hoop), constants AA, BB. The class slides use the radial pressure prp_r (compressive, so pr=−σrp_r=-\sigma_r), the hoop stress σc\sigma_c, constants aa, bb, and radii r1r_1 (inner) and r2r_2 (outer). The two forms are the same equations: a=Aa=A and b=Bb=B.

B5.4 Designing a thick cylinder

Design uses the maximum hoop stress at the inner surface (maximum-principal-stress basis, suited to brittle materials). Set (σθ)max=σallow(\sigma_\theta)_{max}=\sigma_{allow} in (i):

rori=σallow+piσallow−pi\dfrac{r_o}{r_i}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_{allow}+p_i}{\sigma_{allow}-p_i}} (B.14)

Design insights (N 18). (1) As pi→σallowp_i\to\sigma_{allow} the radicand tends to infinity, so no wall thickness can safely contain pi≥σallowp_i\ge\sigma_{allow} in a simple cylinder: compound (shrink-fit) cylinders or autofrettage are used instead. (2) Thickness gives diminishing returns: most of the stress reduction occurs in the first part of the wall, because the high-stress region is at the bore. (3) The thin-wall formula pr/tpr/t under-estimates the maximum stress as t/rt/r grows.

C. Energy methods

C1. Why energy methods

Stress and strain relate forces to deformations through equilibrium and Hooke's law. A third idea is strain energy: the energy stored in a deformed body. Energy methods handle variable cross-sections and complex structures with a single scalar quantity, and lead to deflection formulas (N 20).

Everyday examples. A stretched spring, a bent bow and a twisted rubber band all store energy that comes back when released. Railway couplers and crash barriers must absorb a lot of impact energy without breaking; a bolt whose shank is turned down to the thread-root diameter absorbs more energy at the same peak stress.

C2. Strain energy

Fix a rod BC of length LL and area AA at B and load it at C by a slowly increasing axial force PP. The elementary work of PP over a small elongation dxdx is dU=P dxdU=P\,dx, so the total work, equal to the area under the load-deformation diagram, is the strain energy (N 20).

Strain energy

U=∫0x1P dxU=\displaystyle\int_0^{x_1}P\,dx (C.1). For a linear elastic member P=kxP=kx: UU​=12kx12=\tfrac12kx_1^2​=12P1x1=\tfrac12P_1x_1 (C.2). Unit: N m = joule (J).

Use in impact problems. A body of mass mm moving with speed v0v_0 strikes a rod. If no energy is lost, the maximum strain energy equals the kinetic energy: Um=12mv02U_m=\tfrac12mv_0^2. For a uniform rod Um=σm2AL/2EU_m=\sigma_m^2AL/2E, so σm=mv02E/(AL)\sigma_m=\sqrt{mv_0^2E/(AL)} (N 21). The rod's ability to withstand impact depends on its toughness.
